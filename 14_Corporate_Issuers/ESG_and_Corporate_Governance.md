# ESG and Corporate Governance

## Ownership Structures (14.a)
- **Dispersed** ownership (many small holders) → **principal-agent** (owner-manager) conflict; weak individual monitoring.
- **Concentrated** ownership (controlling shareholder) → **principal-principal** conflict (controlling vs minority shareholders). Can be horizontal (among large holders) or vertical (controlling vs minority).
- Mechanisms: **dual-class shares**, voting caps, pyramids, cross-holdings — separate **control from cash-flow rights**, entrenching insiders.

## Types of Influential Owners (14.a)
Banks, families, sovereign governments, institutional investors, group companies, private equity, foreign investors, managers, board directors. Each brings different incentives/horizons and governance implications (e.g., family/state owners → entrenchment and principal-principal risk; institutions → activism/monitoring).

- **Families → interlocking directorates.** Curriculum definition: individuals sit on the boards of **numerous companies**, "a term called **interlocking directorates**, resulting in **one family controlling multiple companies**" (common in Latin America and similar markets). Two-sided assessment: ✅ principal-agent issues may be **reduced** (owners aligned with the firm's long-run health); ⚠️ hard to recruit quality outside managers, **lack of concern for minority shareholders**, minimal transparency, low management accountability. Net effect: trades a principal-agent conflict for a **principal-principal** one.
- **Group companies → cross-holdings.** Curriculum names **Samsung**: outsized control via **vertical and horizontal cross-holding of shares**, which makes it difficult for outsiders to acquire shares and **increases related-party transactions that disadvantage minority shareholders**. Interlocking boards **+** cross-holdings is the entrenchment combination.
- **Managers/directors owning shares**: aligns insiders with other shareholders, but insiders may also use stakes to protect themselves.

### Director Independence — the definitional test (14.a)

> Curriculum: "When a board member has **no significant remuneration, ownership, or employment relationship with the firm**, the board member is considered independent."

| Disqualifier | Typical form |
|---|---|
| **Remuneration** | consulting fees, advisory retainers, pay beyond normal director fees |
| **Ownership** | material equity stake, or representing a controlling holder |
| **Employment** | current/recent executive of the firm or a related group company |

Independent directors matter most under **dispersed** ownership, where the principal-agent problem is greatest and board monitoring is the key control.

### Is There a Rule Against Cross-Directorships? — No

There is **no curriculum prohibition** on interlocking directorates; it is a **risk factor**, not a bright-line rule. Two things can actually be failed:
1. **The independence test** — the director cannot be *counted* as independent (above), and/or the board falls below a **majority independent**.
2. **"Comply or explain" codes (14.a)** — national governance codes "require firms to either adopt best practices of corporate governance **or explain why they have not**"; **stewardship codes** engage investors to exercise legal rights (UK institutional investors must comply or explain). **The breach is failing to disclose/explain the deviation — the regime regulates transparency, not structure.**

**When cross-directorships become a red flag** (① – ⑤ curriculum-supported; ⑥ – ⑦ standard governance analysis, *not* curriculum text):
① family control spanning multiple companies · ② combined with cross-shareholdings (related-party transactions) · ③ **long tenure** (>10 yrs → "too friendly with management, affecting independence") · ④ non-independent members on the **audit / compensation / nomination** committees · ⑤ **CEO duality** alongside interlocks · ⑥ *reciprocal interlock* (A's CEO on B's comp committee and vice versa — each sets the other's pay) · ⑦ *over-boarding* (too many seats to monitor any properly).

## Evaluating Governance Effectiveness (14.b)
Three official buckets — **board policies, executive remuneration, shareholder voting rights**:
- **Board**: independence, expertise, **CEO duality** (CEO also chair = red flag), committee structure (audit, remuneration, nomination), staggered vs annual elections. **One-tier** board = single board of executive + non-executive directors; **two-tier** = a **supervisory** board overseeing a **management** board (common in Europe).
- **Executive remuneration**: pay-for-performance alignment, clawbacks, equity-based incentives.
- **Shareholder voting rights**: one-share-one-vote vs **dual/multiple-class** shares, voting caps, takeover defenses, related-party transactions, transparency and audit quality.

## ESG Integration (14.c, 14.d)
- **Materiality**: focus on ESG factors **financially material** to the issuer/industry (e.g., emissions for utilities/steel, data privacy for tech, water for beverages). **Governance** factors are fairly **consistent** across firms; **E and S** factors **differ greatly** by industry.
- **Three sources of ESG information**: (1) **proprietary** research from public disclosures (annual/proxy/ sustainability reports, 10-K) — limited by **voluntary, inconsistent** reporting; (2) **ESG data vendors** (MSCI, Sustainalytics) scores/rankings — subjective interpretation; (3) **not-for-profit / NGO frameworks** promoting uniform standards (e.g., SASB).
- Implementation approaches: **negative/exclusionary screening**, **positive/best-in-class screening**, **thematic** investing, **ESG integration** (into forecasts/valuation), **engagement/active ownership**, **impact** investing.
- **Analytical use differs by asset class**: in **equity** analysis, ESG integration both **identifies opportunities** and **mitigates downside risk** (adjust revenue/margins, capex, terminal value); in **fixed income**, it feeds the **credit spread / discount rate** (worse ESG → wider spread, higher yield).

## Exam Traps
- Dispersed ownership → **principal-agent**; concentrated ownership → **principal-principal** (minority expropriation) conflict.
- Dual-class structures separate **control rights from cash-flow rights** — a governance red flag.
- ESG analysis should center on **financially material** factors, not all ESG issues.
- ESG **integration ≠ screening**: integration folds ESG into the valuation/forecast; screening just includes/excludes names. Equity use → opportunities + downside; FI use → **credit spread**.
- **Horizontal** ownership = mutual cross-holdings; **vertical/pyramid** = controlling stakes through tiers of holding companies. **CEO duality** (CEO = chair) raises governance risk.
- **Interlocking directorates are NOT prohibited** — there is no rule to "violate." What fails is the **independence test** (significant remuneration / ownership / employment relationship) or the **comply-or-explain disclosure** obligation.
- Counterintuitive point the exam likes: family control / interlocking directorates can **reduce** the principal-agent conflict while creating a **principal-principal** one. Don't assume every governance concentration is purely negative.
- **Board independence standard: a MAJORITY should be independent** — and the **audit, compensation, and nomination** committees specifically must be "sufficiently independent."
- **Long tenure (>10 years) cuts both ways**: deep firm knowledge vs. resistance to change and becoming "too friendly with management, affecting independence."

## Board Evaluation Checklist (14.b)
1. **Majority independent?** (remuneration / ownership / employment tests)
2. **CEO duality?** (chair = CEO → compromised oversight)
3. **Key committees independent?** (financial reporting, management selection, compensation)
4. **Long tenure** eroding independence?
5. **Interlocking directorates / cross-holdings** concentrating control?
6. Ownership **dispersed** (→ principal-agent) or **concentrated** (→ principal-principal)?
7. **Skills/experience** matched to the firm's specific ESG risks; small, **diverse** boards outperform large homogeneous ones.

Consequences of ineffective governance: reputational damage, difficulty competing, low/volatile stock price, **higher borrowing costs**, decreased profitability → may trigger **shareholder activism**.

## Q&A

### 2026-08-02 — When do interlocking directorates violate the rules?
**Q:** Under what circumstances does a cross-directorship / interlocking directorate breach the rules?
**A:** **There is no rule to breach** — the curriculum treats interlocking directorates as a **risk factor**, not a prohibition. Two things can actually fail. **(1) The independence test:** a director is independent only with "**no significant remuneration, ownership, or employment relationship with the firm**"; the board standard is a **majority independent**, with **audit / compensation / nomination** committees "sufficiently independent." **(2) Comply-or-explain codes:** national governance codes (and the UK Stewardship Code for institutions) require firms to adopt best practice **or explain why not** — so the breach is **failing to disclose/explain**, since the regime regulates *transparency*, not structure. Curriculum definition: individuals sitting on **numerous** boards, "resulting in **one family controlling multiple companies**." Two-sided: it can **reduce** the principal-agent conflict while creating a **principal-principal** one (minority expropriation, low transparency). Red flags: family control across companies, **cross-holdings** (Samsung — related-party transactions), **tenure >10 yrs** ("too friendly with management"), non-independent committee members, and **CEO duality**.

### 2026-06-03 — Principal-agent vs principal-principal conflict
**Q:** How does ownership structure determine the type of governance conflict?
**A:** **Dispersed ownership** (many small holders) → **principal-agent** conflict: owners (principals) vs managers (agents), with weak monitoring because no single holder has incentive to police management. **Concentrated ownership** (a controlling shareholder) → **principal-principal** conflict: the controlling shareholder vs **minority** shareholders, who can be expropriated. Mechanisms like **dual-class shares, pyramids, and cross-holdings** separate **control rights from cash-flow rights**, entrenching insiders — a governance red flag.

### 2026-06-03 — What does "materiality" mean in ESG integration?
**Q:** Should an analyst weigh all ESG issues equally?
**A:** No — focus on factors that are **financially material** to that issuer/industry (e.g., carbon emissions for utilities, data privacy for tech, water for beverages). Material ESG risks feed into forecasts: adjust revenue/margins, capex, **cost of capital**, and terminal assumptions for risks (stranded assets, litigation, regulation) and opportunities. Implementation styles range from negative/positive screening and thematic/impact investing to full **ESG integration** and active ownership.

### 2026-06-04 — ESG integration: equity vs fixed income, and information sources
**Q:** How does ESG integration differ between equity and fixed-income analysis, and where do ESG factors come from?
**A:** In **equity** analysis, ESG integration is used both to **identify opportunities** and to **mitigate downside risk** — analysts adjust revenue, margins, capex, cost of capital, and terminal value. In **fixed-income** analysis, ESG factors flow mainly into the **credit spread / discount rate** (weaker ESG → wider spread, higher required yield, lower bond price). Three information sources: (1) **proprietary** research from public disclosures (annual/proxy/sustainability reports, 10-K) — hampered by **voluntary, inconsistent** reporting; (2) **vendor** scores (MSCI, Sustainalytics) — subjective; (3) **NGO/not-for-profit frameworks** standardizing disclosure (e.g., SASB). Trap: governance factors are fairly uniform across firms; E and S vary greatly by industry.
